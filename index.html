Abstract

People with disabilities meet the web through a browser. Every page they read has been parsed, styled, laid out, and exposed to assistive technology by a browser engine. This paper asks how far today's engines and browsers already go toward making content usable for each person, what public evidence shows about the consistency of that support, and what engineering work would let a user state their needs once and have every page adapted to them.

The paper examines the three engines that underlie mainstream browsing (Chromium's Blink, WebKit, and Gecko) and two emerging engines (Servo and Ladybird). It draws on vendor documentation and source code, W3C specifications, the Interop 2024 accessibility focus area, Web Platform Tests (WPT), W3C ARIA-AT results, PowerMapper screen reader tests, and MDN browser-compat-data. Where no public data exists, the paper says so and labels any judgment as the author's assessment.

Four findings stand out. First, the engines have converged on similar accessibility architectures and agree closely on how they compute roles and accessible names: in the Interop 2024 accessibility focus area, all four tested browsers finished the year at or above 99.7% in preview builds. Second, browsers already ship many user-side aids, including automatic image descriptions, live captions, reading modes with read-aloud, and color overrides, but each is vendor-specific, uneven across engines, and not driven by standardized user preferences. Third, agreement on what browsers compute does not yet mean that people using screen readers hear the same thing in every browser. The W3C ARIA-AT project tests exactly this: it takes correctly built reference examples of common controls, such as tabs, dialogs, and menus, and checks what JAWS, NVDA, and VoiceOver actually convey with specific browsers (at the Candidate stage, JAWS and NVDA with Chrome, and VoiceOver with Safari). Because the examples are reviewed reference implementations, a failure points to the browser, the screen reader, or the way they interact, rather than to the web author. Only 3 of its test plans have reached the Recommended stage, about half are still at the Candidate stage, none yet covers live regions beyond alerts, and results for composite widgets still differ widely between combinations. A new effort, the Accessibility Compatibility Dataset (ACD), is beginning to collect exactly this browser-and-assistive-technology support data for developer tools, though it is in early development. Fourth, no current law or standard requires browsers to provide personalized adaptation. Section 508 and EN 301 549 reach browsers mainly through procurement, and the European Accessibility Act does not list standalone browsers.

The paper proposes a 36-month roadmap divided between shared engine work and browser-product work, with deliverables tied to public test suites. Under stated assumptions (engineering rates derived from published compensation data; staffing and engine shares estimated by the author), the estimated cost is about $17 million to $50 million per engine-and-browser pair at large-company engineering rates (base case about $32 million), and roughly $9 million to $15 million at non-profit rates. Engine work landed once in a shared engine is available to every browser built on it. The base-case annual cost, about $10.6 million, is under 0.004% of annual revenue for Alphabet, Apple, and Microsoft, about 1.0% to 1.6% for Mozilla, and many times Servo's current donation and grant income (Ladybird does not publish a budget).

The paper then makes the commercial case, built on three revenue pools that browser vendors already compete for. The first is AI agents: by Google's published guidance for Chrome, the accessibility tree that screen readers depend on is the primary way AI agents understand a page, other vendors' agent tools also read it, live-site agent success rates remain low, and forecasts of U.S. consumer spending through AI agents by 2030 range from $190 billion to $1 trillion. The second is search, advertising, and commerce from an aging population: Americans aged 50 and over accounted for 56 cents of every dollar spent in the United States in 2018, and disability rises with age. The third is per-seat enterprise and public-sector revenue, where accessibility is a condition of sale in government and education markets, subject to exceptions. Against the three largest reported revenue lines, a change of one-tenth of one percent would equal 10 to more than 26 times the base-case annual cost. The paper closes with a challenge to the browser industry: ten commitments, each measurable from public data, with dates from mid-2027 to the end of 2028.

Key findings

  • Engines agree on computed semantics. Interop 2024 included more than 1,000 tests of accessible name and role computation (Simmons, 2025). By the end of 2024, Chrome, Edge, and Firefox preview builds passed 100% and Safari Technology Preview passed 99.7% (Web Platform Tests, n.d.-a).

  • The architectures have converged. Chromium and Firefox answer assistive technology requests from a cached copy of each page's accessibility tree held in the browser process, and WebKit has been developing an "isolated" tree, readable off the main thread, since 2019.

  • Screen reader output still varies. In PowerMapper's December 2025 tests, overall reliability ranged from 80% (JAWS with Edge; VoiceOver on iOS with Safari) to 92% (NVDA with Firefox). W3C ARIA-AT has 3 test plans at the Recommended stage, all for links.

  • Browsers already help users, but not in a standard way. All four major browsers ship a reading view; Chrome, Edge, and Firefox include read-aloud in it. Chrome and Edge offer opt-in automatic image descriptions for screen reader users. Only Safari exposes in-band audio-description tracks to web content by default, and no engine implements text audio descriptions (<track kind="descriptions">).

  • There is no portable accessibility profile. Some settings sync within one vendor's account, but no browser imports or exports accessibility preferences for use in another browser, and the W3C WAI-Adapt work has had no new technical report publication since January 2023 (W3C, n.d.-a, n.d.-b).

  • Regulation reaches browsers indirectly. Section 508 classes web browsers as platform software and EN 301 549 treats user agents as software, but both apply through procurement. The European Accessibility Act lists operating systems, not browsers. EN 301 549 V4.1.1, published 2 September 2026, adds user-preference clauses for web pages (9.7) and software (11.7) and is awaiting citation in the Official Journal of the European Union.

  • The investment is modest for some vendors and not for others. The base-case annual cost, about $10.6 million, is under 0.004% of annual revenue for Alphabet, Apple, and Microsoft (about 13 to 17 minutes of revenue), about 1.0% to 1.6% of annual revenue for Mozilla depending on the cost rate assumed, and many times Servo's current donation and grant income.

  • The potential return lies in three revenue pools worth hundreds of billions of dollars. AI agents: Chrome's guidance calls the accessibility tree the primary way agents understand a page (Kulikowski & Hablich, 2026), and forecasts of U.S. agent-driven consumer spending by 2030 range from $190 billion to $1 trillion. Search and advertising: Alphabet earned more than $280 billion from online advertising in 2025, and Americans aged 50 and over accounted for 56 cents of every dollar spent in the United States in 2018. Per-seat enterprise and public-sector revenue: Microsoft reports more than 450 million paid Microsoft 365 commercial seats, sold into public-sector markets where accessibility is a condition of purchase, subject to exceptions.

  • A small change would repay the cost many times over. One-tenth of one percent of Alphabet's advertising revenue, Microsoft's Productivity and Business Processes revenue, or Apple's Services revenue would equal about 10 to more than 26 times the base-case annual cost. For Firefox, the work would pay back if it retained or won about 2.1% of monthly active clients.

  • A measurable challenge. Section 13 sets out ten commitments for the browser industry, each checkable from public data, due between June 2027 and December 2028.

1. Introduction

1.1 Why the browser layer

WCAG defines what accessible web content looks like, and decades of guidance, law, and litigation have been aimed at content authors. Yet the most recent WebAIM Million analysis found detected WCAG 2 failures on 95.9% of the top one million home pages, with an average of 56.1 detected errors per page, an increase over the previous year (WebAIM, 2026).

Author's assessment: Content remediation alone has not produced an accessible web.

The browser sits between every author and every user. It already decides how content is rendered, how it is exposed to assistive technologies, and which user settings override author choices. The idea that the user's own agent should adapt content to the person is not new. It appears in early work on universal access, including the "personal interpreter" concept described in 1996 (Laux et al., 1996), and it is the premise of the W3C User Agent Accessibility Guidelines (UAAG 2.0; W3C, 2015) and of the W3C WAI-Adapt work. What has changed is capability: engines now build rich semantic trees, ship machine-learning features, and agree closely on how they compute semantics. This paper asks whether the browser can now carry a larger share of the accessibility load, and what it would take.

An estimated 1.3 billion people, about 16% of the world's population, experience significant disability (World Health Organization, 2022).

1.2 Research questions

  1. What do current browser engines and browsers provide, at the platform and user-interface level, that helps content become usable by people with disabilities?

  1. What public, dated evidence shows how consistently that support works across engines and assistive technologies?

  2. What engineering and standards work would let a user configure their needs once and have the browser adapt any content accordingly, and what would it cost?

1.3 Scope

The paper examines three engines that underlie essentially all mainstream browsing, and two emerging engines:

  • Chromium (Blink rendering engine, V8 JavaScript engine), used by Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Samsung Internet, Opera, Brave, and Vivaldi, and by embedding frameworks such as the Chromium Embedded Framework (CEF), Electron, and Qt WebEngine.

  • WebKit (WebCore, JavaScriptCore), used by Safari on macOS, iOS, iPadOS, and visionOS, and by the WebKitGTK and WPE ports on Linux.

  • Gecko (with the SpiderMonkey JavaScript engine), used by Firefox on desktop and, through GeckoView, Firefox for Android. Firefox for iOS uses WebKit.

  • Servo, a Rust-based engine that has been a Linux Foundation Europe project since September 2023, with Igalia leading development (Linux Foundation Europe, 2023).

  • Ladybird, an independent engine (LibWeb, LibJS) developed by the non-profit Ladybird Browser Initiative, which has targeted 2026 for its first alpha release (Ladybird Browser Initiative, n.d.).

In StatCounter's worldwide data for September 2026, Chrome (66.36%), Safari (18.27%), Edge (6.04%), Firefox (2.80%), Samsung Internet (2.31%), and Opera (1.77%) together accounted for about 97.6% of page views (StatCounter, 2026). StatCounter counts browsers rather than engines, and it counts Chrome and Firefox on iOS as Chrome and Firefox even though they run on WebKit, so engine share cannot be read directly from these figures. Outside the European Union and Japan, Apple's App Store rules require browsers on iOS and iPadOS to use WebKit. Apple has allowed alternative engines for users in the EU since iOS 17.4 (iPadOS 18) and in Japan since iOS 26.2 (Apple, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Apple's published criteria for those alternative engines include web-platform and JavaScript conformance thresholds but no accessibility requirement.

1.4 Method and evidence

Every factual statement in this paper was checked against a source read between 8 October 2026 and the date of release, with preference for primary sources: W3C specifications and test dashboards, browser vendor documentation and source code, regulatory texts, and company filings. Sources and access dates appear in the References. Three kinds of statement are distinguished:

  • Measured: results from public test suites (Interop, WPT, ARIA-AT, PowerMapper) or compatibility data (MDN browser-compat-data, caniuse), cited with the date of the run or report.

  • Documented: features and behaviors stated in vendor documentation or visible in published source code.

  • Author's assessment: judgments about significance, priority, or likely effort. These appear in separate, boxed notes headed "Author's assessment", so readers can always tell the author's interpretation apart from verified fact and weigh it accordingly.

The paper does not report new user testing. Its cost and return figures are estimates built from stated assumptions and public financial data, and Section 12 shows the arithmetic so readers can substitute their own.

1.5 Terminology: content conformance and user agent conformance

WCAG applies to web content (W3C, 2024). Browsers are not evaluated for WCAG conformance, although the browser's own interface can be, and Section 508 applies WCAG 2.0 to software user interfaces (U.S. Access Board, 2017, E207.2). The W3C guideline written for browsers is UAAG 2.0, which is a W3C Working Group Note from December 2015, not a Recommendation (W3C, 2015). The working group that produced it has closed, and W3C has said it does not plan to advance UAAG 2.0 and intends to include user agent considerations in future guidelines work (W3C WAI, 2024a). Several gaps identified in this paper correspond to UAAG 2.0 success criteria that were specified in 2015 but are not consistently implemented. Section 9 maps them.

2. Engine architecture and accessibility foundations

2.1 The accessibility tree

Each engine builds an accessibility tree from the Document Object Model (DOM) and computed styles. Each node carries a role, an accessible name and description, states, properties, and relationships. Native HTML semantics are mapped according to the HTML Accessibility API Mappings (HTML-AAM), ARIA semantics according to the Core Accessibility API Mappings (Core-AAM), and names and descriptions according to the Accessible Name and Description Computation (AccName) (W3C, 2026a, 2026e, 2026f).

Engines may omit, or mark as ignored, nodes that carry no semantics, such as a div or span with no role, name, or focusability. Their children remain exposed under the nearest included ancestor. The exact rules are engine- and platform-specific and are not fully specified. When a generic element is exposed, Core-AAM maps its generic role to platform roles such as UIA Group and AXGroup (W3C, 2026a). role="none" removes only an element's own semantics, and browsers ignore it on elements that are focusable or carry global ARIA attributes; aria-hidden="true" and display: none remove the whole subtree. Construction is lazy and incremental: Chromium, for example, builds accessibility objects on demand and sends batched updates after layout (Chromium Project, n.d.-a).

2.2 Platform accessibility APIs

Browsers expose the tree to assistive technologies through operating system APIs.

Windows. Microsoft Active Accessibility (MSAA) was introduced as a platform add-in for Windows 95 (Microsoft, n.d.-a); a December 1997 Microsoft message to the W3C WAI Interest Group describes the MSAA 1.1 redistributable and its inclusion in Windows 98 (Oppermann, 1997). Microsoft describes UI Automation (UIA) as a newer, more capable technology and continues to support MSAA for backward compatibility (Microsoft, n.d.-a). IAccessible2 (IA2) extends MSAA. It was developed by IBM and contributed to the Free Standards Group in December 2006; the Free Standards Group merged with OSDL in 2007 to form the Linux Foundation, which now hosts the specification (Updegrove, 2006; Linux Foundation, n.d.). Since Chrome 138 in 2025, Chromium has exposed native UIA by default, the result of a joint Microsoft Edge and Google effort, while keeping its MSAA and IA2 implementation (Beaudry, 2025). NVDA continues to use IA2 by default with Chromium; an NVDA maintainer has described Chromium's UIA implementation as weaker than its IA2 implementation (NV Access, 2025). Firefox implements MSAA and IA2 and also includes a native UIA provider (Mozilla, n.d.-a).

macOS. Browsers implement AppKit's NSAccessibility protocol, the "AX API" in W3C mapping specifications (Apple, n.d.-c).

iOS, iPadOS, and visionOS. Apps expose accessibility through UIKit's UIAccessibility, not NSAccessibility (Apple, n.d.-d). VoiceOver is common to all Apple platforms, but the APIs differ.

Linux. Chromium and Firefox implement ATK, which is bridged to the AT-SPI2 D-Bus protocol. WebKitGTK and WPE WebKit have used AT-SPI2 directly, without ATK, since 2022 (WebKit Bugzilla, 2022). Orca is the principal Linux screen reader. Sun Microsystems engineers, working with Mozilla, built much of Firefox's early Linux accessibility support in the mid-2000s (Mozilla, n.d.-b).

2.3 Converging architectures

Assistive technologies query platform APIs largely synchronously, while modern browsers render content in sandboxed processes. All three major engines have adopted, or are developing, similar approaches:

  • Chromium builds the tree in the renderer, serializes batched updates to the browser process, and answers assistive technology requests from that cached copy without blocking on the renderer (Chromium Project, n.d.-a).

  • Firefox re-architected its accessibility engine ("Cache the World") so the parent process holds a cache of every content process's tree. It shipped on Android in Firefox 102, on Windows and Linux in Firefox 112, and on macOS in Firefox 113 (May 2023); Mozilla reported improvements of up to 20 times in worst cases (Mozilla Accessibility Team, 2023).

  • WebKit has developed an "isolated" accessibility tree since 2019, a read-only copy that macOS assistive technology requests can query off the main thread, with work continuing through 2025 (WebKit Bugzilla, 2019, 2025).

Author's assessment: This convergence matters for the roadmap. A cached, serializable tree in a privileged process is the natural place to apply user-preference-driven transformations and to repair semantics, because it is already where engines reconcile what content says with what assistive technology receives.

3. Engine and browser profiles

3.1 Chromium

Blink builds the accessibility tree in the renderer process. Code in content/renderer/accessibility serializes batched tree updates to the browser process, where frame trees are combined and cached. Platform implementations derive from a common abstraction in ui/accessibility/platform (Chromium Project, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Chrome DevTools can replace the DOM tree in the Elements panel with a full-page accessibility tree, and its Accessibility pane shows computed properties for each node (Google, 2026a).

Chromium also contains a chrome.automation API that exposes the accessibility tree to extensions. It is a private API restricted to an allowlist of extension IDs and Google component extensions, such as ChromeOS's ChromeVox screen reader; it is not available to ordinary Chrome Web Store extensions (Chromium Project, 2026a). Public extensions can inject user-origin CSS through chrome.scripting.insertCSS with origin: "USER" (Google, n.d.-a).

3.2 WebKit and Safari

On macOS, WebKit implements NSAccessibility; on iOS, iPadOS, and visionOS it implements UIKit accessibility. VoiceOver's rotor lets users move through web content by element type, using structure that WebKit exposes. Safari's Web Inspector shows, for a selected element, how it is exposed to assistive technologies, and its Audit tab includes a default accessibility audit (Apple, n.d.-e; WebKit Project, 2022). Safari is the only one of the four major browsers that exposes in-band alternate audio tracks, including audio-description tracks, to web content by default (HTMLMediaElement.audioTracks); Chromium and Gecko expose that API only behind flags (MDN, 2026). WebKit source code shows that Apple builds read the operating system's preferred audio characteristics when selecting tracks (WebKit Project, 2026); this is a reading of source code, not a tested result.

3.3 Gecko and Firefox

Mozilla's accessibility work was under way at Netscape by early 2001, with Netscape engineer Aaron Leventhal as its public lead, and Mozilla announced its first accessibility alpha, with MSAA support and keyboard access, in December 2001 (Leventhal, 2001a, 2001b, 2001c). In 2007 the Mozilla Foundation funded NV Access, the developer of NVDA, to improve support for rich web content in Firefox (Hecker, 2007). Firefox's Accessibility Inspector shows the full accessibility tree and can check pages for contrast, keyboard, and text-label issues, simulate color-vision deficiencies, and show tabbing order (Mozilla, n.d.-c).

3.4 Servo

Servo began exposing web-content accessibility in early 2026 using AccessKit, gated behind an accessibility_enabled preference. Each webview exposes its own tree, which the embedding application attaches to its interface tree; on Linux the result reaches Orca through AT-SPI, and incremental tree updates were added in mid-2026 (Servo Project, n.d.-b, 2026a, 2026b). Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency has invested €545,400 in Servo for 2025–2026, covering accessibility, embedding support, and maintainability (Sovereign Tech Agency, n.d.).

3.5 Ladybird

In January 2025, W3C staff member and Ladybird contributor Mike Smith reported that Ladybird was passing nearly all accessibility-related WPT tests (Faulkner, 2025). Those tests check computed roles and names, not platform API output. A pull request titled "Add screen-reader support: Orca and VoiceOver" proposes exposing a tree built in Ladybird's WebContent process through NSAccessibility on macOS and Qt's AT-SPI2 bridge on Linux (Ladybird Browser Initiative, 2026). As of 9 October 2026, its merge status could not be confirmed from the public page.

Author's assessment: New engines are the clearest opportunity to design personalization into the architecture from the start, and also the most resource-constrained (see Section 12).

Several conclusions follow:

  • Repair already happens at the user's side. Chrome and Edge describe unlabeled images on request, and Chrome runs text recognition on scanned PDFs for screen reader users (Google, 2024). These features do not change the page's markup, so authors remain responsible for text alternatives under WCAG 1.1.1.

  • Text-spacing control exists only inside reading views. Users can adjust line, letter, and word spacing in Chrome, Edge, and Firefox reading views, but not on arbitrary pages without an extension or a user style sheet.

  • Audio description is the least supported media feature. No engine implements text-based <track kind="descriptions">; each has an open implementation bug (MDN, 2026).

  • Developer tools are already substantial. Chrome, Edge, and Firefox show full accessibility trees and simulate vision deficiencies, and Safari offers an accessibility audit. None of them previews what a screen reader would announce.

  • Each feature is configured separately, per browser. No feature is driven by a standardized description of the user's needs.

5. Public evidence on consistency

No public test suite rates browsers against WCAG, because WCAG applies to web content; a browser's own interface can be evaluated (Section 1.5), but that is not what these suites measure. What public suites measure is narrower: whether a browser implements a feature that authors rely on, what it computes and exposes to accessibility APIs, and what a particular screen reader announces with a particular browser. Tables 3 and 4 therefore report only measured or documented results, never a rating of a browser's "WCAG support".

5.1 Interop 2024 and Web Platform Tests

Interop is a cross-browser effort in which browser vendors agree on focus areas and track pass rates on shared tests. Accessibility was a scored focus area only in 2024. It covered computation of accessible names and roles for ARIA roles and HTML elements (Simmons, 2024). Accessibility testing has continued as an investigation in 2025 and 2026, aimed at expanding platform-API testing (Web Platform Tests, n.d.-c).

5.2 Assistive technology output

Two sources report what screen readers actually convey with specific browsers.

Why this matters. A browser can compute the right role and name and still fail the user, because what a person actually hears depends on how the browser passes that information to the operating system and how the screen reader interprets it. Readers wary of ARIA should note that ARIA-AT does not test whether web authors use ARIA well. It uses correctly built reference examples from the W3C ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, so a failure shows that even correct code is not yet conveyed consistently, which web authors cannot fix on their own.

W3C ARIA-AT tests ARIA Authoring Practices Guide examples with JAWS, NVDA, and VoiceOver. As of 8 October 2026, 3 test plans had reached Recommended (all link examples, November 2025), 23 were Candidate, 4 were Draft, and 15 were in research and development, including combobox and landmarks. There is no test plan for tree or treegrid, and none for live regions other than role="alert". Candidate reports cover JAWS and NVDA with Chrome and VoiceOver with Safari only, so they cannot compare Gecko with Chromium (W3C ARIA and Assistive Technologies Community Group, 2026). Selected Candidate results, which the project marks "Unapproved Report", are shown in Table 4.

PowerMapper publishes screen reader compatibility tests across about 195 test cases. 6. Post-source accessibility

6.1 Framework

Post-source accessibility refers to changes made to content after it leaves its origin and before or during use. The W3C Accessibility at the Edge Community Group's Final Report distinguishes source-authorized changes, "controlled or approved by the website owner", from user-authorized changes, "added by the user, without website involvement or approval" (W3C Accessibility at the Edge Community Group, 2025). The author of this paper is a co-author of that report. Pages are composites: the median website uses 21 third parties on mobile and 23 on desktop (HTTP Archive, 2021), so no single author controls everything a user receives.

6.2 Browser-native post-source capabilities

Section 4 described the user-authorized capabilities browsers already ship. Two of them are often described inaccurately. User style sheets, frequently cited as a universal way for users to override author styles, are natively available only in Safari on macOS and, behind an advanced preference, in Firefox; Chromium removed them in 2014 (Chromium Project, 2013), so Chrome and Edge users need an extension. Caret browsing, sometimes described as a Firefox and Edge feature, is also available in Chrome.

6.3 Extensions

Extension APIs let third-party tools inject CSS, modify the DOM, and add ARIA attributes. Chromium extensions can inject user-origin CSS; the private chrome.automation tree API is not available to them (Section 3.1). Extensions include contrast and color tools, reading aids, text-to-speech tools, and overlay products that add accessibility widgets to pages. Their effectiveness varies, and the paper does not evaluate individual products.

6.4 Automatability

The Accessibility at the Edge Community Group's Final Report assesses how far each post-source capability can be automated, using four labels: "In many cases", "In some cases", "In no significant way", and "Does not apply" (W3C Accessibility at the Edge Community Group, 2025, section 1.7.3). The report defines "In many cases" as automation that can "successfully remediate and verify outcomes across a broad set of websites." These ratings are the group's expert judgment: the report does not describe a test corpus or measurements, and a W3C Community Group report is not a W3C Standard. Table 5 therefore sets the group's rating for selected capabilities beside independent published evidence and the author's own view.

Two patterns stand out. First, the evidence supports automated detection far more strongly than automated repair: in a 2017 test by the UK Government Digital Service, ten automated tools together found 71% of 143 known barriers on a test page, and 29% were found by none of them (Duran, 2017). Second, where content must be interpreted, as with images, form labels, headings, and speech, independent studies show useful but imperfect results that still need human review.

7. ARIA as a post-source remediation strategy

7.1 Purpose and the first rule of ARIA

WAI-ARIA defines roles, states, and properties that convey semantics when native HTML cannot. WAI-ARIA 1.2 has been a W3C Recommendation since June 2023, and ARIA 1.3 is a Working Draft (W3C, 2023, 2026b). The widely cited "first rule of ARIA use" comes from the W3C document Using ARIA, not from the ARIA specification: "If you can use a native HTML element or attribute with the semantics and behavior you require already built in, instead of re-purposing an element and adding an ARIA role, state or property to make it accessible, then do so." W3C retired Using ARIA as a Discontinued Draft on 24 February 2026 and now points readers to the ARIA Authoring Practices Guide (APG) (W3C, 2026c).

7.2 How browsers implement ARIA

Browsers parse ARIA attributes, integrate them into the accessibility tree, apply the specifications' rules for invalid or prohibited usage, map them to platform APIs, and fire events when states change. They do not validate whether an author's use of ARIA is appropriate. Per Core-AAM 1.2, role="button" maps to ROLE_SYSTEM_PUSHBUTTON in MSAA and IA2, the Button control type in UIA, ROLE_PUSH_BUTTON in ATK/AT-SPI, and AXButton in the macOS AX API. A button with a defined aria-pressed value keeps ROLE_SYSTEM_PUSHBUTTON in MSAA and the Button control type in UIA, but maps to IA2_ROLE_TOGGLE_BUTTON in IA2, ROLE_TOGGLE_BUTTON in ATK/AT-SPI, and AXCheckBox with the AXToggle subrole in the AX API (W3C, 2026a).

7.3 Effective and problematic post-source uses

Post-source ARIA can add missing accessible names, declare live regions, correct roles on elements that clearly function as widgets, and add descriptions. It cannot add behavior: role="button" on a div does not make it focusable or operable. Automated tools may infer purpose incorrectly, composite widget patterns need coordinated roles, states, and keyboard behavior that are hard to inject from outside, and keeping states synchronized with visual state requires knowledge of application logic.

7.4 What the data shows

In the 2026 WebAIM Million, 82.7% of home pages used ARIA (excluding landmark roles). Pages with ARIA averaged 59.1 detected errors, compared with 42 on pages without it (WebAIM, 2026); by the author's calculation, that is about 41% more. WebAIM cautions that this "does not necessarily mean that ARIA introduced these errors," because pages using ARIA also tend to be more complex. The association is consistent with the APG's guidance under the heading "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA" (W3C WAI, n.d.-a).

Author's assessment: For post-source remediation, this suggests that ARIA injection without a reliable understanding of content structure and purpose carries a risk of making matters worse.

7.5 Limitations and browser gaps

ARIA changes only what assistive technology is told. It does not alter appearance, keyboard behavior, or focus management; it offers little for users with cognitive or visual needs that are not met through a screen reader; and, as the APG cautions under "No ARIA is better than Bad ARIA", an ARIA role that is not backed by the matching behavior can leave users worse off than no ARIA (W3C WAI, n.d.-a).

Two gaps are well documented:

  • Live regions. Microsoft's explainer for the ariaNotify() API states that ARIA live regions are inconsistently implemented (Microsoft Edge, n.d.). ariaNotify() shipped in Chrome 141 in September 2025, with partial support on some platforms, and in Firefox 150 in April 2026; Safari does not support it (Google, 2025; Mozilla, 2026a; MDN, 2026). ARIA-AT has no live-region test plan beyond alerts.

  • Complex composites. There is no ARIA-AT test plan for tree or treegrid, and the APG treegrid pattern notes that "some implementations of treegrid may not provide focus to rows" (W3C WAI, n.d.-b).

7.6 Automation and AI

Machine learning, including AI models, can infer structure, describe images, and propose labels, but generated output can be plausible and wrong, and automated systems cannot confirm that a remediation works for assistive technology users. The Accessibility at the Edge report concludes that "while automation and AI can help, we should not expect these can replace humans entirely," and describes human remediation of automatically identified issues, with the content creator's participation, as "an effective and efficient approach to accomplish accessibility" (W3C Accessibility at the Edge Community Group, 2025, section 5.1). Independent evidence is consistent with the first part of that conclusion: automated tools miss a substantial share of known barriers (Duran, 2017), automatic captions usually need editing (W3C WAI, 2024b), and blind users tend to trust generated image descriptions even when they are wrong (MacLeod et al., 2017). No study was found that measures the efficiency of the combined approach the report recommends. Section 10 examines the on-device AI capability that engines have begun to ship, and the concerns raised about it.

8. Regulatory context

This section summarizes what current U.S. and European rules require of browsers. Appendix A gives the full comparison, including points that are frequently misstated. The description is factual and does not take positions on policy.

Section 508 (United States). The Revised 508 Standards (36 CFR Part 1194; compliance required from 18 January 2018) define "platform software" to include web browsers. Section 502.3 requires platform software to provide documented accessibility services, and states that applications that are also platforms "shall expose the underlying platform accessibility services or implement other documented accessibility services." Section 503.2 requires applications to honor platform settings for color, contrast, font, and focus cursor, but exempts applications designed to be isolated from their platform, including web applications. The standards incorporate WCAG 2.0 (U.S. Access Board, 2017), and a search of the Federal Register on 8 October 2026 found no Access Board proposed or final rule to update them since January 2024 (Federal Register, 2026). Section 508 binds federal agencies and reaches browser vendors through procurement. Google and Mozilla publish Accessibility Conformance Reports for Chrome and Firefox, and Apple's macOS report covers Safari features (Google, n.d.-f; Mozilla, 2024b; Apple, 2024).

EN 301 549 (Europe). EN 301 549 V3.2.1 (2021) is the harmonized standard cited under the Web Accessibility Directive for public sector websites and apps (European Commission, 2021a). It treats user agents as software (Clause 11), lists web browsers among platforms, and notes that "for web content, the underlying platform is the user agent." ETSI published V4.1.1, prepared by the CEN/CENELEC/ETSI Joint Working Group on eAccessibility, on 2 September 2026. It moves the web, document, and software baselines to WCAG 2.2 Level A and AA, adds user-preference clauses for web pages (9.7), documents (10.7), and software (11.7), moves authoring-tool requirements to clause 5.10, rewrites the real-time communication clause, and adds annexes mapping the standard to the European Accessibility Act (ETSI, 2026). As of 8 October 2026 it had not been cited in the Official Journal (European Commission, n.d.-a).

European Accessibility Act. The EAA (Directive (EU) 2019/882) has applied since 28 June 2025 to listed products, including consumer computers "and operating systems for those hardware systems", smartphones, and e-readers, and to listed services, including e-commerce, banking, and electronic communications. Standalone web browsers are not listed. Whether a browser bundled with an operating system falls within that operating system's scope is not settled by the Directive's text. No harmonized standard had been cited under the EAA as of 8 October 2026 (European Parliament and Council, 2019; European Commission, n.d.-a).

User preferences in the two frameworks. Section 508 exempts web applications from honoring platform user preferences (503.2). EN 301 549 V3.2.1 notes that for web content the underlying platform is the user agent, and V4.1.1 adds clauses titled "User preferences for web pages" (9.7) and "User preferences" for software (11.7). Neither framework requires browsers to provide personalized adaptation outside procurement.

Author's assessment: Read together, these provisions are relevant to the browser-level personalization proposed here.

8.1 Liability and the user-controlled model

A question that arises whenever software changes what a user sees is whether the party making the change takes on legal responsibility for the result. For browser-level accessibility adaptation, the question has two directions: whether adapting a page shifts a site owner’s legal obligations onto the browser vendor, and whether it relieves the site owner of those obligations. This section offers the author’s assessment, not legal advice; the point is to identify the question, not to resolve it.

The obligations in the frameworks this paper examines attach to content and to the entities that provide it. Section 508 binds federal agencies and reaches vendors through procurement; the Web Accessibility Directive and, prospectively, the European Accessibility Act bind covered bodies and economic operators; and litigation under the Americans with Disabilities Act is brought against the covered entity whose service is at issue. In each case the duty runs to the provider of the content or service, not to the user’s software. WCAG itself applies to web content, not to user agents (Section 1.5), and this paper has already noted that user-side aids such as automatically generated image descriptions do not alter the page’s markup, so authors remain responsible for text alternatives under WCAG 1.1.1 (Section 4).

The user-authorized and source-authorized distinction from the Accessibility at the Edge framework (Section 6) is central here. A user-authorized adaptation is one the user invokes, on their own device, for their own use — the same category as a screen reader, browser zoom, forced-colors mode, or a reading view, all of which users already control today without transferring the site owner’s obligations to the browser vendor. A browser that adapts a page at the user’s request is exercising the user’s long-recognized agency over their own experience, not republishing or warranting the site. On that reading, user-controlled browser adaptation does not move the site owner’s legal duty onto the browser, and it is not designed to.

By the same logic, it does not relieve the site owner either. Because the adaptation happens on the user’s device at the user’s option, and may not occur at all for other users, the content as delivered is unchanged; the author’s obligation to provide accessible content is not discharged by the possibility that some users’ browsers will repair it. Browser-level personalization therefore complements author-side conformance rather than replacing it, and the legal pressure that currently drives content remediation is not removed by it.

Author’s assessment: The liability question is best treated as a design constraint, not an obstacle. A capability that is user-initiated, user-controlled, and executed on the user’s own device sits in the established category of assistive technology and user-agent configuration, where the user, not the software vendor, directs the adaptation and the content author’s duties are unchanged. A source-authorized capability — one a site invokes, or one a vendor applies automatically to all users without their direction — raises different and harder questions, which is a further reason this paper favors the user-controlled model. These are the author’s views; the allocation of liability is ultimately a legal question that merits analysis by counsel and by the standards and policy bodies named in Sections 8 and 12.

10. On-device AI and the engines' stated concerns

The gaps in Section 9 describe work the browser could do. A recent change in browser capability bears directly on whether that work is now feasible: browser engines have begun shipping general-purpose, on-device AI that runs on the user's own machine. This section describes what has shipped, how a browser can act on a page, and the specific, documented concerns that engine and standards participants have raised about the general AI interface. The concerns are real engineering questions; this section sets each beside the narrower, user-controlled accessibility use to which this paper applies them. Nothing here depends on a particular vendor's product; the capability is the point.

10.1 What has shipped

Chrome exposes an on-device language model to code through its Prompt API. As of the Chrome for Developers documentation (last updated 26 August 2026), the Prompt API has been available to Chrome extensions since Chrome 138, and a version for web pages is rolling out in Chrome 148, where the documentation states it "is still being developed" (Google, n.d.-j). The model is Gemini Nano, run locally; the documentation gives no model size but states the feature needs at least 22 GB of free space on the profile's volume, and that the API is available to top-level windows and same-origin iframes but not to Web Workers (Google, n.d.-j). Microsoft's Edge, built on the same engine, has developed a comparable on-device capability; this paper does not independently verify Edge's current status.

Author's assessment: The significance is not the specific product. It is that a mainstream engine now ships a local model that page and extension code can call, on consumer hardware, without sending page content to a server. Whatever one concludes about the general interface, the computational capability that a browser-level accessibility transformation would require is no longer hypothetical.

10.2 How a browser can act on a page

A browser already has complete access to the structures it builds from a page, and can modify them before or as the user receives them. Three layers are available, each described elsewhere in this paper:

  • The DOM. The browser can read and modify the Document Object Model — adding a missing accessible name, correcting a role, inserting a heading. Extensions do this today through user-origin injection (Section 3.1). As Section 7.3 notes, injected ARIA changes only what assistive technology is told; it cannot add behaviour, so a repaired role on a non-interactive element does not make it operable.

  • The accessibility tree. The browser computes the role, name, state, and relationship information it passes to assistive technologies (Section 2.1), and holds it in a cached, serializable copy in a privileged process (Section 2.3). This is the highest-leverage layer: it can repair what assistive technology receives without altering the visible page.

  • The style system. User-origin styles can override author presentation — spacing, colour, contrast, font, reflow — the adaptation described in Gap 2 (Section 9).

A browser-level accessibility transformation therefore combines a capability the browser has always had — reaching into these three layers — with a new one: a local model able to decide what to change. The reaching-in is not new; it is how extensions and assistive technologies already work. What is new is on-device inference capable of informing it. How good is “good enough” is task-dependent — inferring a missing label or simplifying a passage make different demands — and is bounded by the on-device hardware floor discussed in Section 10.1 and by the prototype’s preliminary, still-to-be-tested status noted in Section 10.3; the claim here is that the capability now exists and merits evaluation, not that any one model already meets a defined accessibility threshold across tasks.

Author's assessment: This reframes the feasibility question. The paper's earlier sections show the browser already repairs malformed markup, reflows content, and translates pages. Acting on the accessibility tree or user-origin styles under the user's stated preferences is the same class of operation, now with a local model to guide it.

10.3 Who supplies and controls the intelligence

The post-source framework in Section 6 distinguishes source-authorized changes, controlled by the site owner, from user-authorized changes, made by the user without the site's involvement. The same distinction orders the ways browser-level AI could be applied, along a spectrum:

  • User-supplied intelligence. The user runs a local model they control and directs it at any page. A working example is the “Smart Browser” prototype described by Dr. Gregg C. Vanderheiden — Professor Emeritus at the University of Maryland and founder and Director Emeritus of the Trace R&D Center, and presented at the M-Enabling Summit and to NDIA in 2026: built in roughly one person-month and running entirely on a five-year-old laptop, it already makes non-conforming pages satisfy a substantial share of WCAG success criteria through user-controlled settings. Vanderheiden characterizes these as preliminary, first-pass results that require further testing and acceptance by people with disabilities before such tools are relied upon; his work is supported in part by NSF Grant No. 2312370 and NIDILRR/ACL/HHS Grant No. 90REGE008. (Cited here with attribution, pending his confirmation; the figures and framing are his.) The user brings the model.

  • Vendor-supplied capability. The engine provides a defined, standardized capability that applies accessibility transformations under the user's stated preferences. The engine supplies the model; the user still initiates and controls the adaptation.

Both act on the same three layers in Section 10.2, and both are user-authorized in the Section 6 sense: the user, not the site, decides. Presenting them as a spectrum rather than as competing proposals is deliberate. The case for browser-level accessibility does not depend on which end prevails; it is strengthened by holding both, because the user-controlled property — the safeguard that the concerns in Section 10.4 turn on — holds across the whole range.

10.4 The engines' documented concerns

When the general Prompt API was proposed for the web, engine and standards participants filed specific concerns in public review. These are reproduced here from the original issues, not from secondary summaries, because they bear directly on how a browser-level accessibility capability should be designed. They are documented concerns about a general interface, not settled positions for or against; where a position was proposed or later revised, this paper says so.

  • Mozilla recorded a "negative" position on the Prompt API, stating that "the more general shape of this API has utility, but encourages model-specific behavior that increases interoperability risk," and that developers tuning to one model "would lead to worse behavior with other browsers or models (or sites blocking access)"; Mozilla added that the work "needs much more baking in userland before web APIs are shipped" (Mozilla, n.d.-i).

  • In the WebKit standards-positions review, a reviewer proposed that WebKit oppose the API and raised that "the API has no user consent mechanism for running inference," that on-device inference can mean "the user pays dearly in battery life and device responsiveness, and sites benefit from free compute," that framing on-device inference "as inherently more private is also misleading," and that "model availability remains a fingerprinting vector." The opening review asked whether the interface "would work well with Apple Intelligence or other WebKit browsers" (WebKit, n.d.-a). The WebKit review subsequently resolved to a position of oppose on the Prompt API proposal, and the issue was closed as completed on 26 May 2026; that recorded position concerns this API proposal and not a future browser-level accessibility capability, which remains a separate question.

  • The W3C Technical Architecture Group raised that "on-device models might not meet user, developer, and result expectations," that exposing only two sampling parameters creates "potential interop issues," and that licensing costs "could prevent some independent browsers from shipping a competitive implementation of this API." The TAG first recorded a lack of consensus, then rescinded that resolution and reopened the review, so no final TAG position stands (W3C TAG, n.d.-a).

It is a different architecture, not a repeat — and this paper does not claim automated repair is complete or always correct.

Taken together, the documented concerns do not describe an insurmountable objection to browser-level accessibility. They describe the design of a general AI interface — and, read as requirements, they point toward the same thing this paper proposes: a defined, standardized, user-controlled capability rather than open-ended, site-invoked access to a proprietary model. A further concern, raised in review, is that model-driven adaptation is non-deterministic: the same page could be conveyed differently from one run to the next. This reinforces the case for a defined, versioned, testable capability with conformance tests over open-ended prompting, and for keeping the user in control of when and whether an adaptation is applied. Section 11 sets out the engineering roadmap; Section 12 shows the cost; Section 13 turns both into measurable commitments.

Author's assessment: This is the same method the author has seen work in regulatory settings: take the other side's stated concerns seriously and on the record, and show that the specific proposal meets them. Here the concerns are not even settled positions; most are about a general interface, and the accessibility use answers them by being narrower, user-initiated, and user-controlled. What remains is not a technical barrier but a decision about whether to build it.

11.2 Three revenue pools

Browsers are free to users, so any return would arrive through the revenue a browser drives. User-agent accessibility touches three revenue pools that together run to hundreds of billions of dollars. Figures below are marked as reported (company filings or official statements) or forecast (third-party projections, with publisher and date).

Pool 1: AI agents and agentic commerce

Size (forecast). Forecasts of U.S. consumer spending that AI agents will initiate, influence, or complete by 2030 range from $190 billion to $385 billion (Morgan Stanley, 2025), through $300 billion to $500 billion (Bain & Company, 2025), to "$900 billion to $1 trillion" of "orchestrated revenue" in U.S. retail goods, and $3 trillion to $5 trillion globally (McKinsey & Company, 2025). These are transaction volumes, not revenue that accrues to browser vendors, and they rest on adoption assumptions stated by each forecaster.

Monetization (reported). Vendors already charge for browser agents. Chrome's auto browse is available to Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers in the United States and India (Google, n.d.-i). Microsoft retired Copilot Mode in Edge in May 2026 and made its agent feature, "Browse with Copilot," part of Microsoft 365 Premium for subscribers in the United States (Microsoft, 2026d), a plan announced at $19.99 a month (Microsoft, 2025b). Opera Neon is sold at $19.90 a month (Opera, 2025). The audiences are large: the Gemini app passed 1 billion monthly users (Google, 2026c); ChatGPT reported more than 900 million weekly active users and more than 50 million consumer subscribers (OpenAI, 2026); and Microsoft 365 Copilot passed 30 million paid seats (Microsoft Corporation, 2026a).

Reliability is the constraint (reported). In a study of 300 tasks on 136 live websites, human evaluators found that most agents succeeded about 30% of the time and the best 61.3% (Xue et al., 2025; preprint). On the WebArena benchmark, by comparison, humans succeeded 78.24% of the time and the best agent in the original paper 14.41% (Zhou et al., 2023).

The link to accessibility (reported). The Chrome team states that "a well-formed accessibility tree is the primary way AI agents understand your page" (Kulikowski & Hablich, 2026), and Lighthouse now includes an "Agentic Browsing" category that checks, among other things, that interactive elements have programmatic names (Kulikowski & Hablich, 2026). Google's guidance for agent-friendly websites calls the accessibility tree "a high-fidelity map" for agents (Kulikowski & More, 2026). Microsoft's Playwright MCP server operates the browser through "structured accessibility snapshots" (Microsoft, n.d.-c), and Chrome DevTools MCP's page snapshot is "based on the a11y tree" (Google, n.d.-g). OpenAI told publishers that its Atlas browser agent used "ARIA tags—the same labels and roles that support screen readers" (OpenAI, n.d.-a); OpenAI has since deprecated Atlas, which was scheduled to stop working on 9 August 2026, and is moving browser-based agent capabilities into ChatGPT and Codex (OpenAI, n.d.-b). The WebArena research benchmark supports several page representations, and its baseline agents use the accessibility tree (Zhou et al., 2023). An industry study by Merj, a web research company, swapped the ARIA labels of a Save and a Cancel button: one agent clicked Cancel first in 25 of 25 runs, while two others went to Save. Averaged across agents, success on an icon-only button with no accessible name was about 34% in November 2025 and about 45% in March 2026 (Zecchini, 2026). That study is not peer-reviewed and its dataset is unpublished, and the study calls the accessibility-tree explanation "the most plausible mechanism rather than an established one." Not every agent relies on the accessibility tree, and no peer-reviewed study yet isolates accessibility quality as a cause of agent success.

Author's assessment: Agent reliability on real websites is what stands between vendors' agent subscriptions and the forecast transaction volumes, and the accessibility tree is the input vendors themselves say agents rely on. Engine work that makes the tree complete, correct, and fast is therefore agent-product work. One percent of the lowest 2030 forecast is about $1.9 billion of transactions a year.

Pool 2: Search, advertising, and the longevity economy

Vendor revenue (reported). Alphabet earned more than 70% of its $402.8 billion of 2025 revenue from online advertising (Alphabet Inc., 2026), that is, more than $280 billion (author's calculation). Apple's Services net sales were $109.2 billion in fiscal 2025, at a 75.4% gross margin, and include licensing revenue from Google whose amount Apple does not disclose (Apple Inc., 2025). For Apple, the court record in United States v. Google LLC reports Google payments of about $20 billion in 2022 (U.S. Department of Justice, 2026b). Mozilla's 2024 royalties were $498.2 million (Mozilla Foundation, 2025).

The users (reported and forecast). Americans aged 50 and over contributed $8.3 trillion to the U.S. economy in 2018, and "fifty-six cents of every dollar spent in the United States in 2018 came from someone 50 or older" (Terrell, 2019). AARP's global study with Economist Impact estimated that people aged 50 and over accounted for $35 trillion of consumer spending in 2020 and forecast $96 trillion by 2050 (AARP, 2022). Working-age U.S. adults with disabilities had $674.3 billion of disposable income in 2024 (American Institutes for Research, 2026). About 830 million people worldwide were aged 65 or over in 2024, 10.2% of the population (World Bank, 2026); in the United States the share was about 18.9% on 1 July 2025 (U.S. Census Bureau, 2026, Table 1; author's calculation), and in the European Union 22.0% (Eurostat, 2026a). Disability rises with age: in the United States, 24.3% of people aged 65 to 74 and 45.9% of those aged 75 and over report a disability (U.S. Census Bureau, 2024). WHO estimates that at least 2.2 billion people have a near or distance vision impairment (World Health Organization, 2026), and that hearing loss affects more than 1.5 billion people (World Health Organization, 2021).

The gap (reported). Most older adults are online, but fewer transact: 90% of U.S. adults aged 65 and over used the internet in 2025 (Pew Research Center, n.d.), yet in the European Union in 2025, 55% of internet users aged 65 to 74 bought goods or services online, against 78% of all internet users (Eurostat, 2026b). In 2021, 15% of U.S. adults with a disability said they never go online, against 5% of those without one (Pew Research Center, 2021).

Author's assessment: No published source measures how much search, advertising, or commerce revenue comes from older users or users with disabilities. What the figures do show is scale: the users whose experience browser accessibility most affects are a large and growing share of the population every browser serves, and of its spending, and at Alphabet's advertising base one-tenth of one percent is more than $280 million a year.

Pool 3: Per-seat enterprise and public-sector revenue

The revenue (reported). Microsoft reported more than 450 million paid Microsoft 365 commercial seats (Microsoft Corporation, 2026b). Its Productivity and Business Processes segment had fiscal 2026 revenue of $140.0 billion and operating income of $83.9 billion, a 59.9% operating margin (Microsoft Corporation, 2026a; author's calculation). Edge for Business is included with Microsoft 365 plans (Microsoft, n.d.-d). Chrome Enterprise Core is offered at no cost, and Chrome Enterprise Premium is listed at $6 per user per month (Google, n.d.-h).

Accessibility is a condition of sale (reported). U.S. federal acquisitions of ICT "shall meet the applicable ICT accessibility standards" unless an exception or exemption applies (Federal Acquisition Regulation, n.d.), and federal guidance tells vendors that "it is not voluntary to complete an ACR if you wish the government to consider purchasing your product" (U.S. General Services Administration, n.d.-b). Google publishes Accessibility Conformance Reports for Chrome, ChromeOS, and Workspace for that purpose (Google, n.d.-f). In the European Union, Directive 2014/24/EU requires that technical specifications for procurement intended for use by natural persons take into account accessibility criteria for persons with disabilities or design for all users, except in duly justified cases (European Parliament and Council, 2014, Article 42). EU public authorities spend about €2.6 trillion a year, about 15% of EU GDP (European Commission, n.d.-b), and the U.S. budget for civilian federal IT was about $67.9 billion in FY2026 (Bracken & Mitchell, 2026). Under the Department of Justice's ADA Title II rule, state and local government entities, including public schools and public colleges and universities, must make web content and mobile apps conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA, subject to stated exceptions, by 26 April 2027 for entities with a total population of 50,000 or more, or 26 April 2028 for smaller entities and special district governments (U.S. Department of Justice, 2026a).

Precedent (reported). In 2010, the Departments of Justice and Education wrote jointly to college and university presidents, after settlements over a pilot of an inaccessible e-book reader: "It is unacceptable for universities to use emerging technology without insisting that this technology be accessible to all students" (U.S. Department of Justice & U.S. Department of Education, 2010).

Enforcement is uneven (reported). In GSA's FY2025 government-wide assessment, "inclusion of ICT accessibility in acquisition processes is high but enforcement is low" (U.S. General Services Administration, 2026). In its FY2024 assessment, 50% of reporting entities said they regularly, frequently, or almost always obtain Accessibility Conformance Reports for all products they buy (U.S. General Services Administration, 2024).

Author's assessment: Accessibility does not create this per-seat revenue, but in public-sector and education markets it is a condition of keeping it, and the ADA Title II dates bring state and local governments, public schools, and public universities into scope in 2027 and 2028. At Microsoft's Productivity and Business Processes scale, one-tenth of one percent of revenue is $140 million, or about $84 million of operating income at the segment's average margin.

A related market signal. U.S. federal procurement guidance contemplates testing by an independent third party as one way to assess products (U.S. General Services Administration, n.d.-a), and a market for accessibility testing and remediation services exists. This paper does not size that market or rely on it.

Author's assessment: Third-party accessibility spending suggests that buyers value accessibility outcomes, and enterprise browser offerings are one general avenue through which browser vendors could serve that demand. (The author's employer provides digital accessibility products and services; see Disclosures.)

15. Conclusions

Browser engines now share a common foundation: converged architectures, near-complete agreement on computed semantics, and a growing set of user-side aids. What they have not built is a standard way for a person to state their needs once and have every page adapted to them.

The case for this work can be made in commercial terms. Section 12 shows that the base-case cost is minutes of annual revenue for the three largest vendors, that the work touches revenue pools worth hundreds of billions of dollars (AI agents, search and advertising from an aging population, and per-seat enterprise and public-sector sales), and that for Mozilla it pays back if the work retains or wins about 2.1% of Firefox's monthly active clients. Section 13 turns that case into ten dated commitments that anyone can check.

Author's assessment: The obstacles are not mainly technical. They are the absence of a shared preference model, of consensus tests for assistive technology output, of a maintained user agent guideline, and, for the smaller engines, of funding. Each of those is something browser and engine teams, standards groups, accessibility specialists, and funders can act on together.
