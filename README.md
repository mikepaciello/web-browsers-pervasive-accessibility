## Abstract

People with disabilities meet the web through a browser. Every page they read has been parsed, styled, laid out, and exposed to assistive technology by a browser engine. This paper asks how far today's engines and browsers already go toward making content usable for each person, what public evidence shows about the consistency of that support, and what engineering work would let a user state their needs once and have every page adapted to them.

The paper examines the three engines that underlie mainstream browsing (Chromium's Blink, WebKit, and Gecko) and two emerging engines (Servo and Ladybird). It draws on vendor documentation and source code, W3C specifications, the Interop 2024 accessibility focus area, Web Platform Tests (WPT), W3C ARIA-AT results, PowerMapper screen reader tests, and MDN browser-compat-data. It proposes a 36-month roadmap, makes the commercial case across three revenue pools browser vendors already compete for, and closes with ten measurable commitments for the browser industry.

## About the author

Michael G. Paciello has worked in digital accessibility for more than 40 years. He began his career at Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) in 1982 and later served as Executive Director of the Yuri Rubinsky Insight Foundation, a partner organization at the 1997 launch of the W3C Web Accessibility Initiative. He is a longtime member of the W3C, the author of *Web Accessibility for People with Disabilities*, and the founder of The Paciello Group (TPG), Open Access Technologies (OAT), and WebABLE.com (recently donated to Knowbility).
